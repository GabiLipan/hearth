Hearth · Home and Reports

Three books, three questions

The books are the right model. What sits on top of them asks all three the same question — and, until this week, got two different answers to it depending on which page you were standing on.

Our household

Who put money in, how much of it was a decision, and how much just happened at a till?

Mine

Of what I earned, how much went to us — deliberately, and as spending that ended up ours?

Everything

How do the two relate, and do their figures actually reconcile?

✓ Fixed on this branch

Why the same month printed two different figures

bookTotals and spendsIn deliberately reach outside a book's own accounts for one row type: household shopping bought off a personal card. That is the whole point of paid_for_household — the row is the household's spending, and it lives in an account the household book is not made of.

So both functions have to be handed everything the device can see. Reports did. Home narrowed the list by account first, which switches that branch off with no error and nothing on either screen to say which of them is the odd one out.

Now both Home hands the aggregates the whole list and filters its row list with showsInBook — which also gives Recent activity back your own shopping.

Three more divergences fell out of tracing it:

WhereWhat
Home's flow split from every row, totals from the narrowed one. The "You put in" band was clamped against a contributions figure missing everything bought on a card, silently.
Reports, by year The split was asked for the selected month whatever the period was. Eleven months of well-attributed money quietly became "Put in — not sure by whom".
Budgets Counted paid-for-household in no book at all — while its six-month "typical" column, computed by a different rule, did count it.

All four are one discipline: anything that adds money up takes the unscoped list and selects for itself; anything that lists rows filters with showsInBook. The tests now pin the trap as arithmetic — a narrowed list against a whole one — so it fails rather than drifting back.

Our household Proposed

Put in on purpose, or spent extra

The household book can tell you £3,934 was paid in. It cannot tell you that £134 of that never went near the joint account. Today the only route to that fact is hovering one band of the flow diagram.

Both halves matter, for different reasons. Money moved across is a decision: a figure we agreed, arriving every month. Money spent off a personal card is an accident of which card was in the wallet — and it is the half that quietly turns into somebody being owed.

Mine Proposed

How much of mine went to ours

The personal book already draws a "To the household" band, so this is nearly there. What it does not do is separate the two halves — and they are exactly the two halves worth separating: £2,000 I decided to send, and £90 that happened to me at a till.

Everything Proposed

Stop flattening, start explaining

Today "Everything" is the other two books poured into one pool: the same donut, the same monthly bars, the same flow, with the household and personal accounts added together. It answers no question the other two do not answer better — and its income figure genuinely is not the two of them added up, with nothing on screen to say why.

But the relationship is exactly describable, and two of its three identities are exact:

Left all.net == ours.net + mine.net always, no exceptions Spending all.spend == ours.spend + mine.spend − bought for us from an account you don't hold Money in all.income == ours.income + mine.income − what crossed between us

So Everything should stop being a fourth filter and become the view that explains the other two.

Money moved between our books is counted once in each book and in neither under Everything — the two legs are the same event, so counting either would be counting it twice. That is why Everything's income is not the two figures above it added together. What is left always adds up exactly.

It does not assert that the numbers match. It shows the arithmetic — including the one line that does not add up, and why. Every cell drills to the rows behind it.

The "bought for us on a card you can't see" row is not a rounding note. It is Sam's £44: household spending, in an account this device holds no grant on, of which exactly one row is published. It belongs in the household book and in no account here. Left out of the table it is a £44 discrepancy the reader has to find; in the table it is a fact about the privacy model.

And the crossing, as a picture

The flow diagram in four columns rather than three — stages of the money's journey, which is genuinely what happened to it. Columns two to three are the crossing.

Between our books

March · every ribbon is as wide as the money it carries.

One ribbon Mine → Ours carrying contributions moved plus bought-directly, one Ours → Mine for anything taken back out, and straight-through ribbons for everything that stayed where it landed. No links within a column, so it fits a column-based layout cleanly.

What that one costs, honestly

This is the largest item here and the only one that touches shared geometry. FlowNode gains a column; layoutFlow generalises from in | hub | out to group by column, stack each on the one shared scale, ribbon between adjacent columns. Today's behaviour becomes the case [in, hub, out] — which is what sankey.test.ts should assert, so every existing graph is provably unaffected. It can be deferred without blocking the bridge card.

All three

Build order

  1. Who paid in. A new card plus two ContributionSplit fields. All the data already exists.
  2. How the books add up. A new card plus bookBridge. The identities are already pinned in tests.
  3. To the household, split in two. Two BookTotals fields, one partsOf call, four segments in SalaryStack.
  4. Between our books. The layoutFlow generalisation — the only shared-geometry change.
  5. Split by book. A bookSplitByCategory and a bar variant for the Everything breakdown.

One and two are self-contained cards over data that already exists, and between them they answer all three of the complaints this started from. Four is worth doing only if the picture above is the picture you want.
